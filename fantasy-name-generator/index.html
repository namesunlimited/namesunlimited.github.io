What makes a good fantasy name?

A good fantasy name is easy to say, fits the culture it belongs to, and is clearly different from every other name in your cast. Beyond that, a few things are worth checking:

  • Sound. Soft, flowing sounds (l, r, s, vowels) tend to read as graceful; hard stops (k, g, d, t) tend to read as rugged or hostile. Many fantasy settings lean on these associations, so using them makes a name feel familiar.
  • Role and personality. A scholar, a thief and a warlord rarely share the same kind of name. Decide what the name should suggest before you pick one.
  • Readability. If a reader or a player cannot say it, they will skip it. Avoid stacking apostrophes and unusual letter groups unless the culture really needs them.
  • Memorability. Two or three syllables with a distinctive first letter are easiest to remember. Avoid giving two major characters names that start with the same letter.

How do fantasy names fit a fictional world?

Names fit a world when they follow the same sound rules as the culture they come from. If your elves use soft vowels and your dwarves use hard consonants, readers will sense the difference without being told. Keep one convention per people: for example, dwarves might take a given name and a clan name built from a craft or a deed, while another culture might use only a single name or a title.

Names for places and institutions work the same way. Once you have named a character, you can name the places they come from with the city name generator, or a training ground with the school name generator.

How do you use generated names in a D&D or RPG campaign?

You can use any name you like at your own table, whether you write it D&D or DnD. The names here are fantasy-inspired and are not official content from Dungeons & Dragons or any other game. A simple workflow works well:

  1. Choose the race closest to your character and generate 24 names.
  2. Shortlist three, and say each one out loud at the pace you would use in play.
  3. Check that the name suits the character's background and is not too close to another player's character.
  4. For quick non-player characters, use single names and keep the longer full names for important ones.

If you need a name for a whole character rather than a fantasy race, the character name generator offers genre-based options as well.

Can I use these names in a published book or game?

Common names are generally not protected by copyright, but a name that belongs to a well-known character can raise trademark or confusion issues, and this is not legal advice. Some names in these lists are also used by existing characters in books, games and films (for example, Thorin and Gimli appear in Tolkien's work). Before you publish, search the name you plan to use and change it if it points to a famous character.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes a good fantasy name?

A good fantasy name is easy to pronounce, suits the culture and role of the character, and is different enough from other names in the story. Sound patterns such as soft vowels for graceful peoples and hard consonants for rugged ones help names feel consistent.

How can I make a fantasy name sound unique?

Take a generated name and change one element: swap the surname, shorten the first name, or alter a vowel. Combining a first name from one roll with an epithet from another is a quick way to get a combination you will not see elsewhere.

Can I use these names for D&amp;D characters?

Yes, you can use any name at your table. They are fantasy-inspired names for typical fantasy races and are not official Dungeons & Dragons material.

Are single names or full names better?

Single names are quicker and work well for minor characters. Full names with a clan name or epithet add background and suit main characters, especially dwarves, orcs and nobles.
